CSHE Resources#

Generative AI is now widely accessible to students across disciplines. Rather than attempting to eliminate its use, we should be reimagining assessment to develop critical skills, support academic integrity, and reflect real-world contexts.

These suggestions are aligned with the University of Melbourne CSHE Assessment Guide
Download the full guide here

Rethink Assessment Purpose and Alignment#

  • Clarify what your assessment is really measuring (skills, knowledge, thinking processes).

  • Align tasks with your Intended Learning Outcomes (ILOs) and make sure they still assess what matters, even if students use AI.

  • Focus on higher-order cognitive skills (e.g. evaluation, synthesis, application) where possible.

“Would using AI help or hinder a student’s learning of the core skill this assessment is designed to evaluate?”

Design for Authenticity and Real-World Relevance#

  • Make assessments more authentic by mirroring real-world practices or scenarios where AI tools might be used responsibly.

  • Use context-rich tasks with discipline-specific data, recent or localised issues.

  • Encourage critical reflection on the use of AI tools in professional settings.

e.g. “Use an AI tool to generate a first draft, then critique its accuracy and suggest improvements.”

Incorporate Process, Not Just Product#

  • Emphasise the thinking and development process, not just the final output.

  • Use staged tasks (e.g. annotated drafts, mind maps, version histories).

  • Include student reflections on how they approached the task and what role AI played.

“Upload your AI prompt history and explain how you refined your response over time.”

Embrace Metacognition and Self-Regulation#

  • Ask students to justify their approach, identify their learning gaps, and reflect on decision-making.

  • Use learning journals, annotated artefacts, or video walkthroughs to surface their thinking.

“Why did you structure your argument this way? How did you verify any information produced by AI?”

Use Peer Review, Collaboration and Dialogue#

  • Design opportunities for students to engage with each other’s work.

  • Peer review and collaborative activities promote shared understanding and critique, which are difficult to outsource to AI.

“Provide feedback on your peer’s AI-generated draft before submitting your final version.”

Include AI-Awareness in Academic Integrity Practices#

  • Be transparent about when and how students are allowed to use genAI in assessments.

  • Provide clear examples of appropriate and inappropriate uses.

  • Consider asking students to declare or reflect on how they used AI.

“Complete the AI Use Declaration form as part of your submission.”

Assess More Than One Mode or Medium#

  • Explore multimodal assessments (e.g. combining writing with oral or visual elements).

  • Use live or interactive components like simulations, Q&A sessions, or presentations that are less susceptible to automation.

“Present your findings live and respond to questions from your peers.”

Where to From Here?#

  • Start small: redesign one task to include critical AI use or focus on thinking processes.

  • Collaborate with colleagues and share ideas or challenges on adapting assessments.

  • Keep up with university guidelines and AI capabilities as they evolve.

Generative AI can be a challenge—but also an opportunity to enhance how we assess deep learning, integrity, and real-world readiness.

Adapted from the University of Melbourne CSHE Assessment Guide (2023).
