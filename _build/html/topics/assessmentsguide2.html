SAMR Model#

Using the SAMR Model to Strengthen Assessments in the AI Era#

Generative AI poses new challenges to academic integrity, particularly for traditional assessment tasks that are easily outsourced or automated. The SAMR Model (Substitution, Augmentation, Modification, Redefinition) provides a practical framework to reimagine and reinforce assessments, making them more resistant to AI misuse and better aligned with deeper learning.

What is the SAMR Model?#

The SAMR Model, developed by Dr. Ruben Puentedura, describes different ways technology can be used in education:

  • Substitution – Technology replaces traditional tools, no change in task.

  • Augmentation – Technology improves task efficiency or accuracy.

  • Modification – Task is significantly redesigned.

  • Redefinition – Task is transformed into something previously inconceivable.

When applied with integrity in mind, the SAMR Model helps shift assessments away from static, easily AI-generated responses toward active, contextualised, and process-driven tasks.

Example Assessment Task: Analytical Essay#

Original Task:
“Write a 1,000-word essay analysing how climate change affects agricultural production in Australia.”

This task is vulnerable to being completed entirely using generative AI tools. Here’s how we can progressively rework it through SAMR to build AI resilience:

Substitution#

Task: Students type their essay in a word processor instead of handwriting.

Limitations: No change to task design. Still easily completed by pasting AI-generated text.

Takeaway: Substitution alone does not meaningfully reduce AI misuse.

Augmentation#

Task: Use a collaborative document editor (e.g. Google Docs) to write the essay, with version history enabled and shared with the teacher.

Students are asked to write their analytical essay in a collaborative document editor (e.g. Google Docs, Microsoft Word Online), and must:

  • Share the live document with their teacher from the beginning of the task.

  • Enable version history so all edits and drafts are automatically tracked.

  • Avoid pasting in large pre-written sections (this should be outlined clearly in your task instructions).

  • Include brief comments or reflections (as annotations or footnotes) explaining key choices in structure, sources used, or challenges encountered.

Why This Approach Helps

  • Version history makes the process visible

    • Collaborative tools track each change with timestamps and editor names. This allows teachers to:

      • Monitor how a student’s writing develops over time.

      • Identify sudden insertions that may indicate AI-generated content.

      • Compare early drafts and final versions to assess authenticity.

  • Discourages last-minute AI use

    • Knowing their drafting history is visible, students are less likely to paste AI-written answers.

  • Enables formative support

    • Teachers can intervene early if students are struggling or going off-track.

  • Promotes writing as a process

    • This approach values development and critical thinking, not just the polished product.

Suggested Instructions for Students

“You must write your essay using Google Docs (or another shared editor), and share the live document with your teacher before beginning. Your version history must remain visible and unedited throughout the drafting process. Submissions without a visible drafting trail may not be accepted. Please annotate key decisions or changes in comments or footnotes.”

This approach helps surface the student’s thinking process, while increasing transparency and accountability—making assessments harder to complete using generative AI alone.

Impact: Introduces process visibility. Teachers can monitor drafting over time and flag suspicious changes.

Why it helps: Begins to surface the how, not just the what. Makes AI copy-paste behaviours more detectable.

Modification#

Task: Write your analysis in response to a localised scenario or dataset provided just-in-time (e.g. rainfall patterns in Gippsland 2024). Support your analysis with annotated evidence from the source provided during class.

The idea is this: instead of students writing about a general, widely-known topic (like climate change impacts), you give them something specific, recent, and contextual — ideally something AI tools won’t have detailed, pre-trained knowledge about, such as:

  • Real rainfall data collected in Gippsland (a region in Victoria) during 2023.

  • A new government policy that was released last month.

  • A case study or news article from a local source (not in global datasets).

  • Custom graphs or tables that only exist in your LMS or class materials.

By using these types of sources, you:

  • Reduce the chance that students can rely on ChatGPT or other tools to generate full answers.

  • Require students to interpret and engage with unique material, which increases originality and reduces plagiarism.

Impact: Redesigns the task to include contextual analysis and source annotation that can’t easily be outsourced.

Why it helps: AI struggles with time-sensitive, localised, or data-specific inputs—especially when students must show how they interpret them.

Redefinition#

Task: Prepare a short oral presentation or screencast responding to a previously unseen set of visual or textual sources (e.g. graphs, government reports, news extracts), released during the assessment session. Students must explain and critique how climate impacts are represented, making links to regional agricultural challenges.

Impact: Creates a dynamic, multimodal task that is hard to automate and easy to personalise.

Why it helps: Requires real-time thinking, verbal synthesis, and visible reasoning—skills not easily faked or generated.

Applying SAMR to Strengthen Assessment Integrity#

Use the SAMR model to shift tasks away from static, text-based products by:

  • Requiring in-the-moment responses, personalisation, or unique data inputs.

  • Emphasising process transparency (drafts, annotations, recordings).

  • Designing live, interactive, or multimodal elements that reveal student thinking.

The SAMR model can help future-proof your assessments—making them not only richer and more engaging, but also more resistant to AI misuse.
